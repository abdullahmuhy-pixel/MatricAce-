Physical Sciences Paper 1 vs Paper 2: What's Tested and How to Prepare for Both

📅 Updated 2025 ⏱️ 9 min read 🎓 Grade 10–12 · CAPS

Physical Sciences is a subject many students find intimidating because it covers two completely different disciplines — Physics and Chemistry — each with its own logic, vocabulary, and problem-solving approach. Understanding this split is the first strategic step toward improving your marks.

The Split: Paper 1 Is Physics, Paper 2 Is Chemistry

Both papers are 150 marks and 3 hours long in Grade 12. The divide is clear:

  • Paper 1: Mechanics, waves, electricity, electromagnetism, and photoelectric effect — all Physics
  • Paper 2: Chemical change, electrochemistry, chemical equilibrium, acids and bases, organic chemistry, and industrial chemistry — all Chemistry

Students who are "science people" often do well in one and struggle in the other. Identify early which paper you're stronger in, and make sure you don't let the other one collapse.

Paper 1 — Physics Topics Breakdown

Mechanics: Vectors, Newton's Laws, Momentum (~50 marks)

The foundation of Paper 1. Newton's three laws, free body diagrams, net force calculations, friction, momentum (p = mv), impulse-momentum theorem (FΔt = Δp), and conservation of momentum. These topics appear every year and are consistently worth 40–55 marks.

Exam tip: Always draw a free body diagram before attempting any Newton's Laws question. Mark all forces, choose a positive direction, and apply Fnet = ma. Missing this step is the most common reason students lose marks here.

Work, Energy and Power (~20 marks)

Work-energy theorem (Wnet = ΔEk), conservation of mechanical energy (Ep + Ek = constant when no friction), power (P = W/t = Fv). Questions often combine mechanics with energy — be comfortable switching between Newton's Laws and energy methods.

Waves, Sound and Light (~20 marks)

Doppler effect, standing waves, diffraction, and the electromagnetic spectrum. The Doppler effect (frequency change when source moves relative to observer) appears in virtually every Paper 1 and is worth 8–12 marks. The formula is given on the data sheet.

Electricity and Magnetism (~50 marks)

Coulomb's Law, electric fields, circuits with internal resistance (ε = V + Ir), electromagnetic induction (Faraday's Law), AC generators, and transformers. The internal resistance question (finding EMF, terminal voltage, and lost volts) appears every year and is worth 15–20 marks.

Paper 2 — Chemistry Topics Breakdown

Chemical Equilibrium (~20 marks)

Le Chatelier's Principle and equilibrium constants (Kc). Questions ask you to predict and explain shifts in equilibrium when temperature, pressure, or concentration changes. Always state your reasoning: "Adding product shifts equilibrium to the LEFT because the system opposes the change."

Acids and Bases (~20 marks)

Brønsted-Lowry theory (acid = proton donor, base = proton acceptor), conjugate acid-base pairs, pH calculations, strong vs weak acids, and hydrolysis of salts. These questions require careful reading — identify what type of acid/base is involved before calculating.

Electrochemistry (~30 marks)

Galvanic cells, electrolytic cells, standard electrode potentials (use the table given), and writing half-reactions and overall cell reactions. The EMF of a standard galvanic cell (Eᶿcell = Eᶿcathode − Eᶿanode) appears consistently.

Organic Chemistry (~35 marks)

Naming compounds (IUPAC), functional groups, reactions (addition, substitution, elimination, condensation, hydrolysis), and polymers. Organic chemistry is highly learnable — there are predictable patterns and a finite number of reaction types to memorise.

Strategy: Organic Chemistry questions in Paper 2 often test the same 6–8 types of reactions repeatedly. Master these: halogenation, hydrogenation, hydration, dehydration, esterification, saponification, and cracking. Know the reagents, conditions, and products for each.

Industrial Chemistry (~10 marks)

The Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃), the Contact process for sulphuric acid, and the Chlor-alkali process. These are well-defined and predictable — learn the conditions, products, and industrial significance of each.

Sample Questions

Grade 12 · Paper 1 · Mechanics (3 marks)
A 5 kg object accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 4 seconds. The net force acting on the object is:
✅ a = Δv/t = 20/4 = 5 m/s². Fnet = ma = 5 × 5 = 25 N. Always find acceleration first, then apply Newton's 2nd Law.
Grade 12 · Paper 2 · Equilibrium (2 marks)
For the reaction N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) + energy, increasing the temperature will:
✅ The forward reaction is exothermic (releases energy). Le Chatelier: increasing temperature favours the endothermic (reverse) direction to absorb the extra heat. Equilibrium shifts LEFT, decreasing NH₃ yield.

The Most Common Mistakes in Physical Sciences

  • Not drawing free body diagrams — leads to missing forces and wrong net force calculations
  • Mixing up Paper 1 and Paper 2 formulas — check which data sheet applies
  • Forgetting units — marks are often allocated for correct units separately from the calculation
  • Not defining a positive direction — in momentum and Newton's Laws questions, always state which direction you're taking as positive
  • Misreading Le Chatelier questions — read carefully whether the question asks about equilibrium position, rate, or the value of Kc
